## 1 Identify needs — How do you identify the educational needs of your students?

I begin with the information legitimately available to me: handover, the Student Support File, prior attainment, attendance, agreed accommodations and advice from relevant staff. I compare that with classroom observation, student work, checking understanding and student voice. I identify strengths as well as needs and access barriers, because a diagnosis alone does not tell me how that learner will present in my classroom.

## 2 Learner profile — What do you look for in a learner profile?

A learner profile brings together strengths, needs, aspirations, interests, prior attainment and the context in which the student learns. It can include a communication profile, sensory profile, attendance, preferred supports and current evidence. I use it to guide decisions rather than to create a fixed label, and I update my understanding as individual progress becomes visible.

## 3 Student Support File — How do you use a Student Support File?

The Student Support File is not the support; it informs the support. I identify the student's strengths, priority needs, agreed short-term target, reasonable accommodation and strategies that have worked. I translate that into classroom decisions about clear routines, instructions, questioning, scaffolding, processing time and checking understanding. I also contribute evidence so the plan can be recorded and reviewed rather than remain static.

## 4 Evaluate support — How do you know whether an AEN support is working?

I look for evidence of greater access, participation, individual progress and learner independence. I compare student work, engagement, checking understanding and the level of prompting required, and I use student voice where appropriate. I monitor progress, evaluate impact and record and review with the AEN coordinator or relevant team. If support is not working, I adapt it rather than continuing it because it is written down.

## 5 Independence — How do you make sure support builds participation and independence?

Support should help the learner participate more meaningfully and become less dependent on adult prompting. I may begin with a scaffold, guided prompt or reduced first step, but I deliberately use faded support as competence grows. I keep checking understanding and look for transfer into ordinary classroom tasks. Learner independence is an outcome I plan for, not something I assume will happen automatically.

## 6 Autism — How do you support an autistic student?

For an autistic student I begin with the learner profile and do not make assumptions about ability. I use predictability, clear routines, explicit language, visual supports and appropriate processing time, and I consider the student's sensory profile and communication needs. I reduce unnecessary public pressure while maintaining challenge and high expectations. Autism may require structure or sensory support, not a reduced ceiling on learning.

## 7 ADHD — How do you support a student with ADHD?

For a student with ADHD I make organisation and expectations visible. I use clear routines, chunking, concise explicit language, manageable steps, choice where useful, checking understanding and a movement break where it is part of an appropriate strategy. I consider executive function, attention, inhibition and working memory rather than interpreting every difficulty as deliberate non-compliance. Support is targeted while expectations remain clear.

## 8 Dyslexia — How do you support dyslexia in Maths or Science?

With dyslexia I remove unnecessary literacy barriers without removing the mathematical or scientific demand. I use an accessible layout, visual supports, explicit language, a word bank where useful, a worked example and chunking of reading-heavy tasks. Assistive technology or a reasonable accommodation may also be appropriate. I check whether an incorrect response reflects the concept or difficulty with language access.

## 9 Numeracy — How do you support a student with dyscalculia or significant numeracy difficulty?

For significant numeracy difficulty I make number and structure visible through concrete materials, multiple representations, visual supports and a worked example before expecting fluent symbolic work. I break procedures into manageable stages and use retrieval of important number relationships. I distinguish a numeracy barrier from low potential, monitor progress and keep the mathematical goal worthwhile.

## 10 Communication — How do you support speech, language and communication needs?

For speech, language and communication needs I reduce unnecessary language load without reducing thinking. I use explicit language, visual supports, chunking, a communication profile where available and a guided prompt or alternative response when appropriate. I allow processing time and use checking understanding carefully without putting the student unnecessarily on the spot. The goal is language access to the same worthwhile learning.

## 11 Cognitive load — How do you reduce cognitive load for students who need it?

To reduce cognitive load I activate prior knowledge, limit new information, use chunking and clear sequencing, model with a worked example and use multiple representations where they clarify the idea. I integrate words and visuals and remove unnecessary distraction. A scaffold or part-completed example can support the first attempt, but I use faded support as the learner becomes more secure.

## 12 Executive function — How do you support a student with executive-function difficulties?

When executive function is a barrier, I make the organisation of the task visible. I use clear routines, a visible sequence, chunking, a reduced first step, intermediate goals and checking understanding. For some students a timer, movement break or guided prompt can help task initiation and refocusing. I support the process while continuing to build learner independence rather than organising everything permanently for the student.

## 13 Sensory & regulation — How do you support sensory or regulation needs?

For sensory needs or regulation difficulty I use the sensory profile where available and try to identify the specific access barrier. Predictability, an accessible layout, reduced unnecessary stimulation, choice, processing time or a planned movement break may help. I collaborate with the student and support team, protect dignity and participation, and review whether the adjustment actually improves access rather than assuming it will.

## 14 Anxiety & distress — How do you support a student experiencing anxiety or emotional distress?

With anxiety or emotional distress I prioritise relational safety, predictability and a manageable route back into learning. I may use clear routines, a reduced first step, processing time, choice and calm checking understanding. I do not confuse temporary distress with low ability. I monitor attendance and participation, use student voice, and coordinate support if the pattern is affecting access to learning.

## 15 Attendance — How do you support a student after disrupted attendance or interrupted schooling?

After disrupted attendance I first establish what learning has actually been missed and what has been retained. I use prior attainment, current work and low-stakes retrieval rather than making assumptions. I identify the highest-priority gaps, use a short-term target and coordinated support, and monitor attendance and individual progress. The aim is re-engagement without turning interrupted schooling into a permanent low expectation.

## 16 Learning support — What experience do you have of learning support?

I have worked across mainstream, learning-support, small-group and withdrawal settings with varied learner profiles. I have used concrete materials, visual supports, assistive technology, explicit modelling, worked examples and structured scaffolding. That experience has reinforced that targeted support should connect back to the student's wider curriculum and increase participation and learner independence.

## 17 Team-teaching — How do you use team-teaching effectively for AEN?

In team-teaching, shared planning comes first. We agree the learning goal, likely access barriers, roles and how we will check understanding. During the lesson we can use flexible grouping, targeted guided prompts or an alternative response without one teacher becoming permanently attached to particular students. Afterwards we compare evidence and evaluate impact so team-teaching improves access rather than simply adding another adult.

## 18 Small-group / withdrawal — When is small-group or withdrawal support appropriate?

Small-group or withdrawal support should have a clear purpose that cannot be met as effectively in the mainstream lesson at that time. I link it to an identified short-term target, teach the priority skill explicitly and plan how the learning will transfer back. I monitor progress and review the balance between targeted support and inclusion, because withdrawal should not unnecessarily reduce participation in the wider curriculum.

## 19 SNA — How do you work effectively with an SNA?

The SNA supports access, care needs and participation in line with the student's plan and the school's arrangements; the teacher retains responsibility for teaching and learning. I plan clearly with SNA support, avoid creating unnecessary adult dependence and use faded support where appropriate. We share relevant observations through the agreed team so support contributes to learner independence.

## 20 L1LP / L2LP — How do you plan for students following Level 1 or Level 2 Learning Programmes?

For L1LP / L2LP I begin with the relevant priority learning outcomes and the learner's current needs. I coordinate with the AEN coordinator and support team, use concrete and accessible activities, explicit language, visual supports and achievable short-term targets. I gather evidence of individual progress in communication, numeracy, participation and independence while keeping the student connected to the life of the class.

## 21 Accommodation & technology — How do you use reasonable accommodations or assistive technology?

A reasonable accommodation or assistive technology should remove a genuine access barrier, not lower the learning expectation. I use the agreed accommodation consistently, teach the student how to use the tool and check whether it improves access or independence. Examples might include text-to-speech, alternative response formats or accessible digital materials. I evaluate impact and adjust if the support is not helping.

## 22 Collaboration — How do you collaborate with the AEN team and parents or guardians?

Effective AEN support is coordinated support. I use shared planning with the AEN coordinator, subject colleagues, SNA and other relevant staff, and communicate with parents / guardians where appropriate. I bring classroom evidence and student voice rather than relying only on labels. We agree priorities, monitor progress and record and review changes so everyone is working towards the same meaningful outcomes.

## 23 High expectations — How do you avoid additional needs becoming low expectations?

I actively guard against additional needs becoming low expectations. I begin with strengths, aspirations and interests as well as needs, keep the common learning ambitious where appropriate and provide access through support, not by removing worthwhile challenge. Autism, ADHD, dyslexia or general learning needs do not define a student's potential. I look for evidence of what the learner can do and adjust challenge accordingly.

## 24 Review support — How do you review AEN support over time?

I review support systematically rather than leaving it in place indefinitely. I monitor progress against the short-term target, gather student work, checking understanding, participation data and student voice, and evaluate impact with the AEN coordinator and relevant team. We record and review what should continue, change or fade. The aim is coordinated support that increases access, individual progress and learner independence.
