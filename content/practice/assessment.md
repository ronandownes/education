## Assessment asks: what does the evidence show?

Assessment includes diagnostic, formative and summative processes. The essential question is whether the evidence is valid enough to guide a decision.

## Assessment and Starting Point

## Key Line

**I use assessment diagnostically: establish the starting point, expose the misconception or barrier, respond instructionally, and then check whether the response worked.**

## Establish a Real Starting Point

### How do you establish a real starting point?

I triangulate rather than rely on one source:

- transition/handover information and previous reports
- prior assessment evidence
- short retrieval or diagnostic tasks
- questioning and observation
- student explanation of their thinking

**Interview line:**  
“I don’t assume that a previous mark tells me exactly what a student can do. I use early diagnostic assessment to establish what they know, what they misunderstand and what may be preventing them from showing what they know.”

## Hinge Questions and Misconceptions

### Which hinge question would expose the likely misconception?

A hinge question should be **quick, discriminating and built around plausible wrong answers**.

Example — solving equations:

**Solve:** (3x+5=17)

Possible answers:

- (x=4) ✓
- (x=6) — may have divided before subtracting
- (x=\frac{22}{3}) — may have added 5
- (x=12) — may have stopped after subtracting

The wrong answer gives me information about the student's thinking.

**Interview line:**  
“I design the distractors around likely misconceptions, so the answer tells me not simply whether the student is wrong, but *how* they are thinking.”

## Barrier or Misconception?

### How do you distinguish a reading barrier from a mathematical misconception?

I **change the access route while keeping the mathematics constant**.

For example, I might:

- read the question aloud
- simplify unnecessary language
- highlight key information
- add a diagram or visual representation
- ask the student to explain the mathematics orally

If the student can then complete the mathematics, the original difficulty may have been **literacy or access**. If the mathematical error remains, I investigate the **conceptual misconception**.

**Key distinction:**  
**Remove the possible barrier without removing the mathematical demand.**

## Common Assessment

### When is a common assessment useful, and what can it conceal?

A common assessment is useful for:

- consistency across classes
- identifying patterns across a year group
- collaborative planning
- checking curriculum coverage
- moderation and professional discussion

But the mark can conceal:

- literacy or language barriers
- anxiety or processing difficulty
- different misconceptions producing the same wrong answer
- partial understanding
- students who can explain orally but struggle to record it
- differences in prior opportunity to learn

**Interview line:**  
“A common assessment gives us comparable evidence, but comparability is not the same as diagnosis. I still need to look behind the mark.”

## After the Mark

### What do you do after the mark is recorded?

**The mark is not the end of assessment.**

I ask:

**Evidence → Diagnosis → Response → Recheck**

- What does the evidence tell me?
- Is there a class-wide misconception or an individual need?
- Who needs reteaching, scaffolding or extension?
- Do I need to change my next lesson?
- Can students act on feedback?
- How will I check that the gap has actually closed?

**Strong closing line:**  
“I’m interested in what happens because of the assessment. If the evidence doesn’t change either my teaching or the student’s next action, its formative value is very limited.”
