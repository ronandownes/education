## Differentiation Thesis

### I vary the route, not the learning.

I keep the important learning clear and ambitious.

I then vary access, support, representation, pace, challenge and response so more students can participate successfully and become increasingly independent.

**goal → access → scaffold → represent → assess → adjust → independence**

## What does differentiation mean in your classroom?

### I vary the route to the learning without losing sight of the learning.

I usually keep a common learning intention and vary the route into it.

I think about six main levers: **entry point, scaffolding, representation, pace, challenge and response**.

Sometimes the most useful differentiation is not a different worksheet.

It may be a different representation, a worked example, a concrete model, a sentence stem, a reduced first step or an extension question.

The aim is appropriate access and challenge without unnecessarily separating students from the same learning conversation.

**goal → entry → scaffold → represent → pace → challenge → respond**

🎯 → 🚪 → 🏗️ → 👁️ → ⏱️ → 🧗 → 💬

## What is the difference between accessibility and differentiation?

### Accessibility removes unnecessary barriers before individual differentiation begins.

I first make the lesson as clear and usable as possible for everyone through visible structure, clear instructions, good board layout and manageable first steps.

I explain key vocabulary and use diagrams, models, manipulatives, GeoGebra, Desmos and worked examples where they improve access.

I also try to reduce unnecessary cognitive load and provide more than one way into an idea: verbal, visual, numerical, concrete or symbolic.

Differentiation then adds more targeted variation where particular learners need additional support, a different entry point or greater challenge.

**barrier → access → participate → identify need → differentiate**

🚧 → 🔓 → 🙋 → 🔎 → 🔧

## How does UDL fit?

### Design as much access as possible from the start, then add individual support where necessary.

UDL asks me to anticipate learner variability rather than waiting for students to fail before changing the design.

I think about **engagement, representation, and action and expression**.

That means clear and predictable instructions, multiple useful representations, opportunities for participation and appropriate ways for students to demonstrate understanding.

Good universal design reduces unnecessary barriers for the whole class.

Individual accommodations and targeted supports still matter where universal design is not enough.

**anticipate → design → engage → represent → respond → support**

🔮 → 🧱 → 🙋 → 👁️ → 💬 → 🤲

## How do you use different representations in Mathematics?

### I change the representation when the mathematics is sound but the route into it is not working.

I often move between **concrete, visual or representational, and abstract or symbolic forms**.

That may involve manipulatives, number lines, grids, diagrams, tables, graphs, GeoGebra, Desmos, worked examples or formal notation.

The representation is not decoration; it should expose the mathematical structure students need to notice.

As understanding develops, I connect the representations and move students towards increasing fluency with the formal mathematics.

**concrete → visual → connect → symbolic → fluent**

🧮 → 🖼️ → 🔗 → 🔣 → 🚀

## How do you use scaffolding without creating dependence?

### A scaffold should make the next step possible and then gradually disappear.

I may use worked examples, partially completed solutions, formula boxes, sentence starters, checklists, prompts or guided questioning.

I make the support explicit enough to reduce overload and allow the student to enter the task successfully.

I then fade prompts, reduce structure or increase the level of independence as the student becomes more secure.

The test of a scaffold is not whether the student can complete the supported task; it is whether the student becomes more able to work independently afterwards.

**model → guide → prompt → fade → independence**

👨‍🏫 → 🤝 → 💡 → 🌫️ → 🌱

## How does assessment guide differentiation?

### I differentiate from evidence, not assumption.

I use questioning, mini-whiteboards, observation, student work, hinge questions and retrieval to see who is secure, who is uncertain and where the misconception lies.

Sometimes most of the class is ready to move on while a smaller group needs a quick reteach, a worked example or a prompt.

At other times the evidence tells me that the whole class needs a different explanation or representation.

The same evidence also tells me who is ready for deeper application or extension.

**elicit → interpret → group → support → extend → review**

💬 → 🔎 → 👥 → 🤲 → 🧗 → 🔄

## How do you challenge stronger students?

### Greater challenge should usually mean deeper thinking, not simply more work.

I use questions that ask students to justify a method, compare approaches, find a second method, spot an error, generalise a pattern or solve a reverse problem.

I may also ask students to create an example, test a conjecture or apply the idea in an unfamiliar context.

That keeps stronger students connected to the same core learning while increasing reasoning, abstraction and transfer.

**apply → explain → justify → generalise → transfer**

🛠️ → 🗣️ → ⚖️ → 🔭 → 🔀

## How do you differentiate through mathematical language?

### Language can be the barrier even when the mathematics is within reach.

I explicitly teach key vocabulary, notation, command words and the language of reasoning.

I use annotated examples, visual prompts, sentence stems and structured explanations where they help students express mathematical thinking.

I also distinguish between simplifying the language around a task and oversimplifying the mathematical idea itself.

The aim is to improve access to the mathematics while building students' own mathematical language over time.

**identify language → clarify → model → rehearse → communicate**

🗣️ → 💡 → 👨‍🏫 → 🔄 → 💬

## How can technology support differentiation and accessibility?

### Technology is useful when it changes access, feedback or representation — not simply because it is digital.

I use tools such as GeoGebra and Desmos to make abstract relationships visible and allow students to manipulate mathematical objects dynamically.

Digital tools can also provide immediate feedback, adjustable pacing, repeated practice and alternative ways of engaging with a task.

For some learners, technology can reduce copying demands or support visual organisation and independence.

I still choose the tool from the learning need; technology supports the pedagogy rather than replacing it.

**need → choose tool → represent → interact → feedback → independence**

🎯 → 💻 → 👁️ → 🖱️ → ⚡ → 🌱
