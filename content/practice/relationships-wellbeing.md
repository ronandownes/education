**trust → notice → listen → support → refer → follow up**

## What role do relationships play in learning?

### Trust is not an alternative to expectations; it is what makes expectations workable.

Students are more willing to take intellectual risks, ask for help, accept correction and recover from mistakes when they know the teacher is consistent, fair and respectful. I try to know students as learners, use their names, notice effort and changes in engagement, and create a classroom where high expectations and strong relationships reinforce one another.

## How do you build positive relationships with students while maintaining high expectations?

I am warm, consistent and predictable. I establish clear routines and boundaries, show genuine interest in students, listen carefully, follow through on what I say and treat students fairly. Positive relationships do not mean lowering standards; they make it easier to challenge students because they know the correction is about their learning or behaviour, not about them personally.

## What is the subject teacher's pastoral role?

### The subject teacher often sees the first small signs that something has changed.

I notice attendance, engagement, peer dynamics, sudden changes in work, mood or behaviour and use calm, non-invasive check-ins where appropriate. I contribute observations and pass concerns through the school's tutor, year-head, pastoral, AEN, guidance or safeguarding structures. I support the student, but I do not try to become their counsellor.

## How do you know when a student may need additional pastoral or wellbeing support?

I look for patterns rather than reacting to one isolated incident: withdrawal, repeated lateness or absence, a sudden drop in work, friendship difficulties, changes in behaviour, distress, avoidance or a student no longer participating as they normally would. I check in appropriately, record relevant facts and share concerns through the correct school support structure.

## What would you do if a student became unusually withdrawn or upset?

I would make a quiet, respectful check-in without pressuring the student to disclose anything. I would listen, reassure them that I am taking the concern seriously and decide what needs to happen next. Depending on what emerges, that might involve the tutor, year head, guidance, student-support team, AEN structures or the school's safeguarding procedure.

## How would you respond to concerns about a student's attendance or disengagement?

I would first look for a pattern and gather relevant information rather than assuming the cause. I would speak with the student appropriately, communicate with the relevant pastoral or year-head structure and contribute any classroom evidence I have. The aim is to understand what is getting in the way of attendance or engagement and agree a realistic support or re-engagement plan.

## How would you respond if a student told you they were having friendship difficulties or being bullied?

I would listen carefully, avoid minimising the concern and establish enough factual information to know what needs to happen next. I would not promise confidentiality. I would follow the school's anti-bullying, pastoral and safeguarding procedures as appropriate, record relevant information and ensure the concern reaches the people responsible for following it up.

## How do you create a sense of belonging in your classroom?

I try to make every student feel visible and capable of participating. That means learning names quickly, using inclusive examples and grouping, ensuring quieter students have ways to contribute, noticing effort as well as attainment and avoiding classroom practices that repeatedly expose the same students as unsuccessful. Belonging also depends on consistency: students should know that they will be treated fairly and respectfully.

## What role does student voice play in your teaching?

Student voice helps me understand how students are experiencing the learning rather than assuming I already know. I use questioning, informal check-ins, feedback, reflection and student discussion to identify what is helping, what is getting in the way and where students need more support or challenge. I listen to student views while retaining professional responsibility for the final teaching decisions.

## How do you support student wellbeing without lowering academic expectations?

Wellbeing and achievement are not opposites. A student is more likely to engage with demanding learning when the classroom feels safe, predictable and supportive. I can adjust scaffolding, pacing, access or the route into a task while keeping the underlying learning goal appropriately ambitious.

## How do restorative conversations fit into your practice?

When a relationship has been damaged, I want the student to understand what happened, its impact and how we move forward. I keep the conversation calm and specific, listen to the student's perspective, restate the expectation and agree the next step. The aim is accountability and repair rather than simply winning a confrontation.

## Tell us about a time you deliberately built relationships with a group of students.

In a pastoral role with a large second-year group, I used short student check-in forms covering subjects, homework and wellbeing. The responses gave quieter students a way to communicate concerns and created natural openings for one-to-one conversations. It helped students feel heard and also surfaced practical issues that could then be addressed through the appropriate school supports.

## How would you support a student who was struggling but reluctant to ask for help?

I would reduce the social risk of asking for help by checking in quietly, giving structured opportunities for all students to ask questions and normalising mistakes and uncertainty as part of learning. I would look at the student's work and engagement for clues, offer a manageable first step and involve wider support structures where the difficulty appeared persistent or significant.

## How do you deal with a difficult conversation with a student?

I keep the conversation private where possible, calm and focused on the specific behaviour or issue rather than the student's character. I listen, clarify what happened, explain the impact or expectation and agree what needs to happen next. I avoid getting drawn into an argument and follow up afterwards so the relationship can reset.

## How do you work with parents or guardians around a student's wellbeing or engagement?

I communicate factually and constructively, using specific evidence rather than labels. I explain what I have noticed, listen to relevant context from home and focus the conversation on practical next steps. Where appropriate, I coordinate with the tutor, year head, AEN, guidance or student-support structure so that communication is consistent and proportionate.

## How do you balance confidentiality with the need to share concerns?

I respect student privacy, but I do not promise secrecy. Information is shared only with the appropriate people and only where it is relevant to supporting or safeguarding the student. If a concern reaches a child-protection threshold, I follow the school's safeguarding procedures immediately.

## What would you do if a student made a disclosure that raised a safeguarding concern?

I would listen calmly, avoid leading questions, not promise confidentiality, record the relevant facts accurately and follow the school's child-protection procedures immediately, including reporting to the Designated Liaison Person as required. My role is not to investigate the disclosure.

## How do you contribute to a whole-school culture of wellbeing?

I contribute through everyday relational practice as well as formal structures: consistent expectations, respectful communication, noticing vulnerable students, supporting inclusion, sharing relevant concerns, contributing to pastoral or student-support systems and collaborating with colleagues. A wellbeing culture is built through many small, consistent actions across the school.
