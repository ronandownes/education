      <h2>Bí Cineálta — Procedures to Prevent and Address Bullying Behaviour</h2>
      <p class="resource-meta">Department of Education, June 2024 · replaces the 2013 anti-bullying procedures · in effect from 1 September 2025.</p>

    <div class="summary-level" data-summary-level="1" hidden>
      <h3>1 — One line</h3>
      <p>Bí Cineálta is the current whole-school framework for preventing, identifying, recording and addressing bullying, with student voice, parent engagement, support, follow-up and board oversight built into the process.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="summary-level" data-summary-level="2" hidden>
      <h3>2 — Very short</h3>
      <ul>
        <li>Bullying is targeted harmful behaviour, repeated over time, involving an imbalance of power; a single harmful online post may count where it is likely to be repeatedly shared.</li>
        <li>Schools need prevention strategies, a clear response process, records of bullying incidents and support for all students involved.</li>
        <li>Parents are engaged early, progress is reviewed within 20 school days, and the principal updates the board at every ordinary meeting.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>

    <div class="summary-level" data-summary-level="3">
      <h3>3 — Quick interview recall</h3>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Definition:</strong> targeted behaviour causing physical, social or emotional harm, repeated over time, with a power imbalance.</li>
        <li><strong>Prevention:</strong> whole-school culture, curriculum, policy/planning and relationships/partnerships.</li>
        <li><strong>Response:</strong> listen, establish what happened, involve the student in deciding next steps, act promptly and contact parents.</li>
        <li><strong>Follow-up:</strong> review with students and parents no later than 20 school days after the initial discussion.</li>
        <li><strong>Oversight:</strong> record incidents, provide board updates and review the school policy annually with the school community.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>

    <div class="summary-level" data-summary-level="4" hidden>
      <h3>4 — Short working summary</h3>
      <p>The procedures use a child-rights and partnership approach. They treat prevention, support, oversight and community as connected rather than seeing bullying only as an individual discipline issue.</p>
      <ul>
        <li>Correctly distinguish bullying from other unacceptable behaviour. Non-bullying behaviour still has to be addressed through the Code of Behaviour.</li>
        <li>Pay explicit attention to cyberbullying, racist bullying, disability-related bullying, homophobic/transphobic bullying, sexist bullying and sexual harassment.</li>
        <li>Create a telling environment where students know who trusted adults are and believe reporting will lead to a safe, proportionate response.</li>
        <li>Support the student experiencing the behaviour, the student displaying it and students who witness it.</li>
        <li>Preserve privacy, communicate sensitively, consider age and ability, listen to student views and inform parents early.</li>
        <li>Record the incident and the actions taken, then review progress within 20 school days.</li>
        <li>Use board-level reporting and an annual community-informed policy review to examine patterns and effectiveness.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>

    <div class="summary-level" data-summary-level="5" hidden>
      <h3>5 — Core requirements</h3>
      <p><strong>What counts as bullying?</strong> The core tests are targeted harmful behaviour, repetition and an imbalance of power. Intentional single offline incidents do not normally satisfy the repetition test, although they still require a response. A single harmful online item can meet the repetition element because it can be redistributed repeatedly.</p>
      <p><strong>What must the school have?</strong> A Bí Cineálta policy developed with the school community, a student-friendly version, specified prevention strategies, specified approaches for addressing incidents and support strategies for the students involved.</p>
      <p><strong>What happens when an incident is reported?</strong> Staff establish what happened, listen to those involved, apply the definition carefully, support the student experiencing the behaviour without delay, engage the student displaying the behaviour, involve parents at an early stage and agree actions that are appropriate to the circumstances.</p>
      <p><strong>What happens afterwards?</strong> The teacher records the incident and engagements, reviews progress with students and parents within 20 school days and continues to review where the behaviour has not ceased. Persistent inappropriate behaviour may also be addressed under the Code of Behaviour.</p>
      <p><strong>Who oversees it?</strong> The principal reports numbers, patterns and responses to each ordinary board meeting without identifying students. The policy is reviewed once each calendar year with input from staff, students, parents and the board, or sooner where an urgent review is warranted.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="summary-level" data-summary-level="6" hidden>
      <h3>6 — Prevention → response → review</h3>
      <p><strong>Prevention.</strong> Prevention is embedded across the four wellbeing areas: culture and environment; curriculum/teaching and learning; policy and planning; relationships and partnerships. Schools are expected to identify concrete prevention strategies, including strategies relevant to cyberbullying and identity-based bullying, and to make it easy and safe for students to tell a trusted adult.</p>
      <p><strong>Identification.</strong> Staff apply the definition rather than assuming every conflict is bullying. They consider what happened, where, when and why, and whether the behaviour is targeted, repeated and characterised by a power imbalance. If those criteria are not met, the behaviour may still require action under the Code of Behaviour.</p>
      <p><strong>Immediate response.</strong> The student experiencing the behaviour should feel heard, reassured and involved in the response. Privacy, sensitivity, age, ability and communication needs matter. Parents of those involved are engaged early. Both the student experiencing bullying and the student displaying bullying may need support.</p>
      <p><strong>Choice of approach.</strong> There is no single mandated intervention for every incident. The school sets out the approaches it uses. Restorative practice or mediation should only be used where staff are appropriately trained and the students involved agree.</p>
      <p><strong>Review.</strong> The teacher checks back with the students and parents no later than 20 school days after the initial discussion. If bullying has not stopped, strategies are reviewed and further follow-up is agreed.</p>
      <p><strong>Recording and oversight.</strong> Incidents and reviews are documented. The principal provides regular anonymised board updates. The whole-school policy is reviewed annually and its effectiveness is considered, not merely its existence.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="summary-level" data-summary-level="7" hidden>
      <h3>7 — Detailed staff-facing summary</h3>
      <p><strong>Purpose and stance.</strong> Bí Cineálta replaces the 2013 procedures and moves the emphasis towards a rights-based, whole-school and partnership model. Prevention, support, oversight and community are the organising principles.</p>
      <p><strong>Definition.</strong> Bullying requires targeted behaviour that causes harm, repetition over time and an imbalance of power. Harm may be physical, social or emotional. Power can arise from size, status, ability, peer-group influence, identity, social position or other relational factors. The online environment changes the repetition test because one harmful post can be shared many times.</p>
      <p><strong>Specific forms.</strong> The procedures explicitly recognise cyberbullying and identity-based forms, including racist, disability-related, gender-identity, homophobic/transphobic and sexist bullying, as well as sexual harassment.</p>
      <p><strong>School policy.</strong> Each school develops its policy with the school community and creates a student-friendly version. Prevention strategies and the approaches used to address bullying should be stated clearly so staff, students and parents understand what will happen.</p>
      <p><strong>When a report is made.</strong> Staff should establish the facts fairly, often speaking separately with students at first where a group is involved. The student experiencing bullying is engaged without delay. Their views on how the matter should be addressed are taken seriously. Parents are informed early and communication barriers such as literacy or language are considered.</p>
      <p><strong>Requests for no action.</strong> A request simply to “look out” for a student is handled sensitively, but the school still works with the student on safe next steps and how parents will be informed. A parent may ask in writing that no action be taken, but the school may decide that intervention is necessary.</p>
      <p><strong>Follow-up and records.</strong> Progress is reviewed within 20 school days. Records include the form/type of bullying where known, when and where it happened, engagement dates, views of students and parents, actions taken, review outcomes and the date the behaviour is considered to have ceased. Relevant external supports may also be noted.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="summary-level" data-summary-level="8" hidden>
      <h3>8 — Operational reference</h3>
      <p><strong>Whole-school preparation</strong></p>
      <ul>
        <li>Develop and publish the Bí Cineálta policy with consultation across the school community.</li>
        <li>Create and display a student-friendly policy.</li>
        <li>Identify prevention strategies across school culture, curriculum, policy/planning and relationships.</li>
        <li>Include strategies for relevant forms of cyberbullying and identity-based bullying.</li>
        <li>Make reporting routes and trusted adults clear to students.</li>
      </ul>
      <p><strong>When behaviour is reported or witnessed</strong></p>
      <ul>
        <li>Establish what, where, when and why; speak individually first where a group is involved.</li>
        <li>Apply the definition carefully: targeted harmful behaviour + repetition + power imbalance.</li>
        <li>If it is not bullying, do not ignore it; use the Code of Behaviour or other appropriate supports.</li>
        <li>Listen to and reassure the student experiencing the behaviour, protect privacy and act promptly.</li>
        <li>Engage the student displaying the behaviour and identify the support they may need.</li>
        <li>Contact parents early and consult them on actions.</li>
        <li>Use only interventions suited to the case and already specified by the school. Restorative practice or mediation requires trained staff and student agreement.</li>
      </ul>
      <p><strong>After the initial intervention</strong></p>
      <ul>
        <li>Record the incident and actions taken.</li>
        <li>Review with students and parents within 20 school days.</li>
        <li>If the bullying continues, revise the strategies and set another review point.</li>
        <li>Continue supervision/support even after the behaviour has ceased where needed.</li>
        <li>Where a Student Support File exists, the procedures encourage placing a copy of the record on it; a Student Support Plan should reflect relevant response strategies and supports.</li>
      </ul>
      <p><strong>Governance</strong></p>
      <ul>
        <li>The principal gives an anonymised bullying-behaviour update at each ordinary board meeting.</li>
        <li>The update includes new incidents, ongoing incidents, year-to-date totals and relevant patterns or strategic issues.</li>
        <li>The board and school community review the policy each calendar year, or earlier if a serious matter indicates an urgent review.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>

    <div class="summary-level" data-summary-level="9" hidden>
      <h3>9 — Interview-depth summary</h3>
      <p><strong>The conceptual shift:</strong> Bí Cineálta is not simply a revised sanctions procedure. It frames bullying as a rights, wellbeing and relationship issue requiring prevention, participation, support and governance. The whole school community shares responsibility.</p>
      <p><strong>The definition matters:</strong> teachers should not use “bullying” as a catch-all label. The behaviour must be targeted and harmful, repeated over time and involve a power imbalance. A single offline incident may be serious and actionable without being bullying; a single online post may amount to bullying where repeated sharing is likely. This distinction keeps the response accurate while ensuring harmful behaviour is still addressed.</p>
      <p><strong>Prevention is visible and specific:</strong> schools should be able to point to actual strategies across culture/environment, teaching and learning, policy/planning and relationships/partnerships. The policy should address contemporary forms such as cyberbullying, racist bullying, homophobic/transphobic bullying, disability-related bullying, sexist bullying and sexual harassment. A telling environment and trusted adults are central because students often do not report when they fear retaliation, disbelief or loss of control.</p>
      <p><strong>The response protects agency:</strong> the student experiencing bullying should be listened to and involved in decisions rather than having adults automatically take over. Staff act promptly and sensitively, protect privacy as far as possible, account for age and ability and inform parents early. The student displaying bullying also requires engagement and support; the aim is to stop the behaviour and, where practicable, repair relationships.</p>
      <p><strong>There is no automatic restorative response:</strong> schools choose approaches appropriate to their context. Restorative practice and mediation are conditional: staff need suitable training and students need to agree to the method.</p>
      <p><strong>Follow-up is mandatory:</strong> the teacher returns to the students and parents within 20 school days to determine whether the bullying has ceased and whether the strategies are working. If it continues, the response is revised and monitored. Ongoing support may remain necessary even after the behaviour stops.</p>
      <p><strong>Documentation connects pastoral and organisational support:</strong> incidents, engagements, decisions and outcomes are recorded. Where a Student Support File exists, the procedures encourage the record to be included so support can be coordinated. Records are retained in line with school record-keeping and data-protection requirements.</p>
      <p><strong>Oversight asks whether the policy works:</strong> the principal gives anonymised quantitative and qualitative updates to each ordinary board meeting. The annual review involves the wider school community and considers implementation, patterns, prevention and areas for improvement.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="summary-level" data-summary-level="10" hidden>
      <h3>10 — Detailed working reference</h3>
      <p><strong>Chapter 1 — Context and responsibilities.</strong> Bí Cineálta sits within the wider Cineáltas action plan and a child-rights approach. The procedures emphasise collective responsibility across students, staff, parents, school leaders and boards. Prevention, support, oversight and community are the four organising principles.</p>
      <p><strong>Chapter 2 — Identifying bullying.</strong> The core definition has three elements: targeted behaviour that causes harm; repetition over time; and an imbalance of power. Harm may be physical, social or emotional. Bullying may be verbal, physical, relational, online or identity-based. A single offline incident normally fails the repetition test but remains a behaviour issue; a single online item may satisfy repetition where repeated distribution is highly likely. Staff also need to recognise when behaviour may become a child-protection or criminal matter and follow the relevant procedures rather than treating every case solely as a school-discipline issue.</p>
      <p><strong>Chapter 3 — Impact.</strong> Bullying can affect attendance, engagement, belonging, confidence, wellbeing and relationships. The impact is not confined to the student experiencing it: witnesses and students displaying bullying behaviour may also need support. This underpins the procedures’ emphasis on a coordinated rather than purely punitive response.</p>
      <p><strong>Chapter 4 — School policy.</strong> Every school develops a Bí Cineálta policy in consultation with its community and uses the Department template as its basis. The policy records prevention strategies, including strategies for relevant forms of online and identity-based bullying; it identifies support strategies; and it states how reported incidents will be addressed. A student-friendly version must also be developed and displayed. All bullying incidents are recorded.</p>
      <p><strong>Chapter 5 — Prevention.</strong> Prevention is organised through the four wellbeing areas: culture/environment; curriculum and teaching/learning; policy/planning; relationships/partnerships. Schools should foster respectful relationships, student participation, equality and inclusion and a telling environment. Students need to know who they can approach and to trust that reporting will be taken seriously. Prevention should directly address contemporary patterns such as cyberbullying, racism, homophobic/transphobic bullying, sexism and sexual harassment where relevant to the setting.</p>
      <p><strong>Chapter 6 — Addressing incidents.</strong> The primary aim is to stop the bullying and, where practicable, restore relationships. Staff identify whether bullying has actually occurred by establishing what happened and applying the definition. When groups are involved, individual accounts are generally taken first. Students experiencing bullying should be engaged quickly, listened to, reassured and involved in deciding next steps. Privacy and sensitivity are important, as are age, ability and communication needs. Parents of the students involved are contacted early and consulted. The student displaying bullying behaviour should also be supported to address relational or other needs contributing to the behaviour.</p>
      <p><strong>Requests to do nothing.</strong> A student may ask an adult only to keep an eye on them. Staff should respond empathetically but still work with the student on safe next steps and on how parents will be informed. A parent may request no action, normally in writing, but the school may decide the circumstances require intervention.</p>
      <p><strong>Choice of intervention.</strong> There is no universal method suitable for every case. Schools specify the approaches they use. Restorative practice and mediation are not automatic: they should only be used by trained staff and where the students involved agree.</p>
      <p><strong>20-school-day review.</strong> No later than 20 school days after the initial discussion, the teacher engages again with the students and parents to assess the nature of the behaviour, the effectiveness of the response and the relationships involved. If bullying continues, the strategies are revised and further reviews are scheduled. Where inappropriate behaviour persists, Code of Behaviour strategies may also be considered. Support and supervision can continue after the bullying itself has ceased.</p>
      <p><strong>Recording and student support.</strong> Records cover the form/type of bullying where known, where and when it occurred, engagement dates, the views of students and parents, agreed actions, review outcomes and the date the behaviour is determined to have ceased. External-service engagement can also be noted. Where a Student Support File exists, schools are encouraged to place a copy of the record there; where a Student Support Plan exists, relevant supports and strategies should be incorporated.</p>
      <p><strong>Complaints and supports.</strong> Parents dissatisfied with how bullying has been addressed are directed to the school complaints procedure; further recourse may include the Ombudsman for Children where appropriate. The procedures also signpost supports such as Oide, Webwise, NEPS, the National Parents Council, the DCU Anti-Bullying Centre and Tusla where child-protection concerns arise.</p>
      <p><strong>Chapter 7 — Oversight.</strong> At each ordinary board meeting the principal reports the number of new incidents since the previous meeting, the number still ongoing and the year-to-date total, along with relevant anonymised information on trends, strategies, serious impacts, additional support needs and whether urgent policy review is required. The board minutes the required information without identifying students.</p>
      <p><strong>Annual review.</strong> The school reviews its Bí Cineálta policy once each calendar year with input from the board, staff, students, parents and, where appropriate, the wider community. Student councils and parent associations should be engaged where they exist. The review is meant to test implementation and effectiveness and identify improvements, not merely re-approve the document.</p>
      <p><strong>Interview line:</strong> I would treat Bí Cineálta as a whole-school prevention-and-response framework: identify accurately, listen and protect student agency, involve parents, support all students involved, document and follow up within 20 school days, and use patterns and board oversight to improve prevention.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
